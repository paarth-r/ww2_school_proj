Museum Project Research OutlineTopic: Technological Advances in World War IIOverview: Technological Advances in World War IITopic: Technological Advances in World War IIMain ideas: WW2 saw huge advances in basically every area of military technology. Guns, tanks, planes, bombs, and medicine all changed dramatically between 1939 and 1945. The countries that developed better technology and could produce it in large enough quantities generally had a big advantage.Most important things to know: Technology alone did not win the war. Being able to mass produce weapons and supplies was just as important as inventing new things. Also a lot of the technology developed during the war is still affecting everyday life today.Why it matters: WW2 basically started the modern world in terms of technology. Jet engines, nuclear energy, penicillin, radar, and early computers all either started or got massively developed because of the war.Source 1 (MLA citation): Overy, Richard. Why the Allies Won. W.W. Norton, 1995.How found: Google, searched "why did allies win ww2 technology"Search process: I wanted to know what actually gave the Allies the advantage and technology kept coming up so I looked for a book specifically about that.Strengths: Written by an actual historian, covers multiple countries and types of technology, not just one thing. Lots of specific examples.Weaknesses: It is pretty long and focuses more on the Allies so you don't get as much on German or Japanese technology specifically.New questions raised: Made me wonder how much of it was just having more factories vs. actually better inventions. Could be its own subtopic.Source 2 (MLA citation): Buderi, Robert. The Invention That Changed the World. Simon & Schuster, 1996.How found: Google, searched "ww2 radar history book"Search process: I kept seeing radar mentioned as one of the biggest technological advantages in the war so I looked for something specifically on that.Strengths: Really detailed about how radar actually worked and how it affected battles. Uses interviews with real scientists.Weaknesses: Only covers radar, not other technologies. Some of the science parts are confusing if you don't have a background in physics.New questions raised: The book mentions that radar technology eventually led to microwave ovens which is kind of crazy. Could look more into how war tech became everyday tech.Research:WW2 changed technology in basically every area of warfare at the same time. Radar let the British detect German planes before they arrived, which was a big reason they won the Battle of Britain. The US being able to produce huge amounts of weapons and equipment (like through Lend-Lease) helped the Allies overwhelm the Axis even when German weapons were individually better.Sub-Topic #1: Guns and Infantry WeaponsSubtopic: Guns and Infantry WeaponsMain ideas: The standard infantry rifle changed a lot during the war. The US M1 Garand was semi-automatic meaning soldiers could fire 8 rounds without reloading while most German soldiers had bolt-action rifles. Germany's MG42 machine gun fired so fast the Allies called it 'Hitler's buzzsaw.' New weapons like the bazooka also changed how infantry could fight against tanks.Why it matters: Better infantry weapons meant soldiers could fire faster and more accurately. This directly affected how many people survived battles.Source 1 (MLA citation): Overy, Richard. Why the Allies Won. W.W. Norton, 1995.How found: Google, searched "why did allies win ww2 technology"Search process: I wanted to know what actually gave the Allies the advantage and technology kept coming up so I looked for a book specifically about that.Strengths: Written by an actual historian, covers multiple countries and types of technology, not just one thing. Lots of specific examples.Weaknesses: It is pretty long and focuses more on the Allies so you don't get as much on German or Japanese technology specifically.New questions raised: Made me wonder how much of it was just having more factories vs. actually better inventions. Could be its own subtopic.Research:The M1 Garand is semi-automatic, so a US soldier could fire 8 rounds without reloading while a German soldier with a Kar98k had to manually cycle the bolt after every shot. The German MG42 fired around 1,200 rounds per minute which was about twice as fast as anything the Allies had. The proximity fuze was a small radar inside an artillery shell that made it explode at the right height above a target instead of on impact, which made anti-aircraft guns way more effective.Sub-Topic #2: TanksSubtopic: TanksMain ideas: Tanks improved a lot during the war. Germany's Tiger tank had thick armor and a powerful gun but was slow and broke down a lot. The Soviet T-34 was not as good individually but was cheap and fast to produce. The US made tens of thousands of Sherman tanks. At the Battle of Kursk in 1943 over 6,000 tanks fought each other which was the biggest tank battle ever.Why it matters: Tanks basically replaced cavalry as the main offensive ground force. Whoever controlled the tank battlefield usually won the battle.Source 3 (MLA citation): Zaloga, Steven J. T-34/76 Medium Tank 1941-45. Osprey Publishing, 1994.How found: Google, searched "ww2 soviet tank history"Search process: After reading about tank battles I wanted to understand why the T-34 was considered so important so I searched specifically for that.Strengths: Very specific information on the tank including specs and how it actually performed in battles. The author clearly knows what he is talking about.Weaknesses: Only covers one specific tank so you have to find other sources for German or American tanks. Not much on strategy or the bigger picture.New questions raised: Got me curious about why Germany kept making better individual tanks but still lost. Is quality or quantity more important in war?Research:The T-34 had sloped armor which deflected shells better than flat armor, and wide tracks that worked better in mud and snow than German tanks. Germany's Tiger I had an 88mm gun and 100mm of frontal armor which was basically the best tank in the war one-on-one. But the USSR built over 55,000 T-34s during the war and Germany only made about 1,347 Tigers total. The US made about 49,000 Shermans. So even though German tanks were better individually, the Allies just replaced losses way faster.Sub-Topic #3: AircraftSubtopic: AircraftMain ideas: Planes improved enormously between 1939 and 1945. Fighters like the Spitfire and P-51 Mustang got faster and had longer range. The P-51 could escort bombers all the way to Berlin and back which was a game changer. Germany introduced the Me 262 which was the world's first jet fighter used in combat, about 100 mph faster than anything the Allies had. But it came too late.Why it matters: Air power determined whether you could safely move troops and supplies. Losing air superiority basically meant losing the campaign above it.Source 2 (MLA citation): Buderi, Robert. The Invention That Changed the World. Simon & Schuster, 1996.How found: Google, searched "ww2 radar history book"Search process: I kept seeing radar mentioned as one of the biggest technological advantages in the war so I looked for something specifically on that.Strengths: Really detailed about how radar actually worked and how it affected battles. Uses interviews with real scientists.Weaknesses: Only covers radar, not other technologies. Some of the science parts are confusing if you don't have a background in physics.New questions raised: The book mentions that radar technology eventually led to microwave ovens which is kind of crazy. Could look more into how war tech became everyday tech.Research:The P-51 Mustang originally used an American engine and was mediocre, but when they put in a British Rolls-Royce Merlin engine it became probably the best fighter of the war. Its range of over 1,600 miles meant it could escort bombers all the way to Berlin and back, which changed everything for strategic bombing. Germany's Me 262 jet was faster than anything the Allies had but Hitler kept ordering it converted to a bomber instead of a fighter, which wasted a lot of its potential.Sub-Topic #4: The Atomic BombSubtopic: The Atomic BombMain ideas: The Manhattan Project was the US program to build a nuclear weapon. It involved over 130,000 people and cost about 2 billion dollars. The bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 killed somewhere between 110,000 and 210,000 people and ended the war with Japan. Germany was also trying to build one but never got close.Why it matters: The atomic bomb completely changed what war could look like. It ended WW2 but also started the Cold War nuclear arms race that lasted for decades.Source 4 (MLA citation): Rhodes, Richard. The Making of the Atomic Bomb. Simon & Schuster, 1986.How found: A librarian recommended it when I asked about the Manhattan Project.Search process: I already knew the atomic bomb was the biggest weapons development of the war so I asked a librarian for a good book on how it was actually made.Strengths: Won the Pulitzer Prize. Very well researched, covers the whole history from the science to the actual bombing. Considered one of the best books on the subject.Weaknesses: Super long (900+ pages) and some of the physics is really hard to follow. Mostly about the American program and not much on Germany's atomic research.New questions raised: Raises a lot of questions about whether dropping the bomb was actually the right call. That could be a whole other paper.Research:The first nuclear test was called Trinity, on July 16, 1945 in New Mexico. The Hiroshima bomb (Little Boy) used uranium and was a gun-type design. The Nagasaki bomb (Fat Man) used plutonium and an implosion design which was more efficient. Japan surrendered on August 15, 1945, six days after Nagasaki. The whole Manhattan Project started partly because Einstein wrote a letter to Roosevelt warning him that Germany might be building a nuclear weapon.Sub-Topic #5: MedicineSubtopic: MedicineMain ideas: Medicine is often forgotten when people talk about WW2 technology but it was huge. Penicillin was mass produced for the first time during the war. Before that infected wounds killed more soldiers than actual combat in most wars. Blood transfusions and better field surgery also saved a lot of lives. The military also started taking mental health more seriously with what they called shell shock.Why it matters: Medical advances during WW2 created the modern antibiotic era. Penicillin alone has probably saved hundreds of millions of lives since 1945.Source 5 (MLA citation): Cowdrey, Albert E. Fighting for Life: American Military Medicine in World War II. Free Press, 1994.How found: Google, searched "ww2 medicine penicillin history"Search process: I noticed medicine was not something most people think about when they talk about ww2 technology so I searched for sources on that specifically.Strengths: Written by someone who worked at the Army's medical history office so he had access to actual military records. Covers a lot of different areas of medicine not just penicillin.Weaknesses: Only covers American medicine, so you don't get what was happening medically in other countries. Might be hard to find at a regular library.New questions raised: Made me think about how many soldiers in earlier wars died from infections instead of actual combat wounds. Penicillin basically changed that.Research:Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928 but nobody could produce enough of it to actually use until WW2 made it a priority. By D-Day in June 1944 there was enough penicillin to treat all expected Allied casualties. In WW1 more soldiers died from infections and disease than from actual combat wounds. WW2 also saw the first large scale use of blood plasma transfusions and the mobile surgical hospital concept, where surgeons operated right behind the front lines instead of waiting to get soldiers to a hospital.
